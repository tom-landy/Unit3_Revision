UNIT 3 PODCAST SOURCE PACK: E15 Cracking the 6, 8 and 12-mark questions
BTEC Level 3 National in Business, Unit 3: Personal and Business Finance (Pearson).

=== THE EXAM ===
2 hours, 80 marks. Section A Personal Finance (about a third of the marks, 45 minutes). Section B Business Finance (about two thirds, 75 minutes). Formulas are not given. Questions 5 and 10(d) or 11 are 6 or 8-mark discuss or analyse questions; Questions 6 and the last part of 11 are 12-mark evaluate questions.

=== HOW LEVELS-BASED QUESTIONS ARE MARKED ===
6-mark discuss: Level 1 (1-2) basic description; Level 2 (3-4) some weighing up of pros and cons in context but imbalanced; Level 3 (5-6) well-developed, balanced discussion in context with technical language.
8-mark analyse: Level 1 (1-3) isolated knowledge; Level 2 (4-6) some linkages between factors; Level 3 (7-8) thorough application with balanced analysis linking the figures.
12-mark evaluate: Level 1 (1-3) basic description and generic conclusions; Level 2 (4-6) some application and judgements, some lacking support; Level 3 (7-9) balanced evaluation with supported judgements; Level 4 (10-12) thorough, balanced, coherent evaluation using the data, leading to supported conclusions.

=== EXAMPLE QUESTIONS AND MARK SCHEMES ===
QUESTION (12 marks, topic A4.2): Zayan is a 19-year-old university student. He often uses his agreed bank overdraft to pay his rent and for holidays. He has inherited £15,000 which he wants to invest. His goal is to open his own business within the next two years. He has estimated that opening a business will cost £16,500. Zayan has researched four savings and investment options, shown in the table. (Deposit guaranteed means the value of your investment won't go down and you will get back at least the sum invested.) Evaluate which saving/investment option would be most suitable for Zayan. (Past paper, January 2026)
DATA:
Option | Minimum investment | AER % | Fees | Deposit guaranteed | Period
Stocks and shares ISA | £50 lump sum and £50 per month | 7.8% fixed for 6 months then variable | 1.5% of investment | No | 2 years
Bonds and gilts | £1,000 | 5.06% fixed | 0 | Yes | 4 years
British saving bond | £500 | 4.10% fixed | £100 | Yes | 3 years
Open access cash ISA | £500 | 4.81% variable + 0.71% bonus if no withdrawals in first 12 months | 0 | Yes | 2 years, instant access, 3 withdrawals a year
MARK SCHEME:
Stocks and shares: lowest minimum (£50), but £50 a month may be hard for a student. Highest rate, but only fixed for 6 months. At 7.8% for 2 years he'd earn £1,170 then £1,261.26, beating his target even after 1.5% fees (£225). But his money isn't guaranteed; he could lose his inheritance. Only tied up for 2 years.

Bonds and gilts: £1,000 minimum is fine. 5.06% fixed: £759 then £797.40, so £16,556.40 after 2 years, hitting the target. No fees, guaranteed. But tied up for 4 years, so he can't start the business in 2.

British savings bond: 4.10% earns £615 then about £640, short of £1,500, and £100 fees. Guaranteed, but the 3-year term is too long.

Cash ISA: 4.81% variable; with no withdrawals 5.52%, which would hit the target if the rate holds. Guaranteed, tax-free. Instant access is useful for emergencies as a student, but he may be tempted to spend.

Application: he's 19, a student, needs exactly £16,500 in 2 years, may need money in an emergency.

A strong answer reaches a supported judgement, e.g. the cash ISA balances safety, access and the target, or stocks and shares only if he accepts the risk.

Levels: 10 to 12 thorough, balanced evaluation using the figures with a supported conclusion; 7 to 9 balanced with some support; 4 to 6 some application and judgement; 1 to 3 basic description.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic A4.2): Tia is 28 and works as a supply teacher, so her earnings are often unpredictable. When Tia has paid her utility bills and living expenses each month, she has between £100 and £400 left over, which she would like to save. She has £1,500 in her current account. Her personal goals are to buy her own home and to save money for emergencies and for travelling. Tia has researched the savings options in the table, available through her bank. Evaluate which option would be the most suitable for Tia. (Past paper, January 2024)
DATA:
Account | AER % | Term | Minimum deposit | Maximum monthly deposit | Instant access
Regular saver | 5% variable | 12 months | £1,000 | £200 | Yes
Deposit account | 3.5% fixed | 3 years | £2,000 | £7,000 | No
Cash ISA | 2.75% fixed | 2 years | £1 | £20,000 per year | Yes, but lose 6 months' interest
Current account | 1.5% on credit balances | N/A | £1 | No limit | Yes
MARK SCHEME:
Regular saver: highest rate at 5%, only 12 months, she has the £1,000 to open it, instant access helps if supply work dries up. But the £200 monthly maximum may be too low in months she can save £400.

Deposit account: second highest rate (3.5%) but she needs £2,000 to open and only has £1,500. Money locked for 3 years, so no use if work dries up or she wants to travel. £7,000 monthly limit is plenty.

Cash ISA: 2.75%, can open with £1 straight away, £20,000 limit easily covers her £1,200 to £4,800 a year. Instant access, but she loses the interest if she withdraws.

Current account: lowest rate (1.5%) but already open, no limits, instant access, which suits an unpredictable income.

A strong answer reaches a supported judgement, e.g. the regular saver for up to £200 a month plus the cash ISA or current account for anything above that.

Levels: 10 to 12 thorough, balanced evaluation using the figures with a supported conclusion; 7 to 9 balanced with some support; 4 to 6 some application and judgement; 1 to 3 basic description.

Philip predicts that he will spend £58 per month on dog food once the puppy is fully grown. He has researched the insurance options in the table. (Pre-existing conditions are illnesses, injuries or health conditions that existed before the start of a policy.) Evaluate which option would be most suitable for Philip. (Past paper, June 2024)
DATA:
Provider | Cost per month | Covers | Maximum claim | Vet nurse text-chat | Excess
Shirevale | £10 | Accidents only | £500 per accident, up to £2,000 per year | No | £150
Ashridge | £38 | Accidents only | £5,000 per year | No | £80

Context: Philip is a student so money is tight; food will cost £58 a month; the puppy is a rescue with unknown history, so may have pre-existing conditions; average claim £750; operations £3,000+.

A strong answer weighs cost against cover in Philip's situation and reaches a justified choice.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic D1.2): Gabriella started her organic gardening and landscaping business in January 2025. She wants to expand by renting premises and opening a small garden centre. She enjoys being her own boss and working with staff but finds other aspects of the business, such as negotiating contracts with customers and suppliers, more difficult. She knows the new business will have high fixed costs and that revenue in the first few months may be low until she builds awareness with local people. She has estimated her set-up costs to be £23,000. Option 1: crowdfunding. Option 2: venture capital. Evaluate whether Gabriella should fund the expansion of Gabriella's Garden Services by crowdfunding (Option 1) or venture capital (Option 2). (Past paper, January 2026)
MARK SCHEME:
For crowdfunding:
- No interest, so lower outflows, important as she's worried about high fixed costs.
- She doesn't give away a share, so keeps control and all the profit; she enjoys being her own boss.
- No repayments.
- The campaign is marketing that builds awareness before opening, helping with low early revenue.
- Tests public reaction; an organic USP may appeal to crowdfunding backers.
- A new business may struggle to get a bank loan.

Against crowdfunding:
- If the target isn't reached she may get nothing, though £23,000 is a modest target.
- Time-consuming, so expansion may be delayed.
- Rewards for backers (free plants or services) cost money.
- Platform fees add to the £23,000.

For venture capital:
- Large sums quickly, so she could rent premises straight away.
- Expert guidance; she has one year's experience and struggles with negotiating.
- Networking: contacts for contracts and cheaper stock.
- No monthly repayments, good for cash flow while fixed costs are high.

Against venture capital:
- She gives up equity and some control, which conflicts with enjoying being her own boss.
- Pressure for rapid growth with little experience.
- Possible conflict with the investor.

A strong answer weighs these against Gabriella's situation and reaches a justified conclusion.

Levels: 10 to 12 thorough, balanced evaluation with a supported conclusion; 7 to 9 balanced with some support; 4 to 6 some application and judgement; 1 to 3 basic description.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic D1.2): Farida runs Camping Cuisine, which makes camping stoves, and has operated it for five years. She has raised most of the finance she needed using her own capital, but has an outstanding loan of £15,000 at 12% interest, due to be repaid before the end of 2024. She keeps all the profit she makes. In 2023 the business made a loss of £26,310. One of her packaging machines keeps breaking down and needs replacing at a cost of £25,000. Option 1: a bank loan for £25,000 at 15% interest over five years. Option 2: £40,000 from a venture capitalist in return for 50% of the profit for the next 10 years; £40,000 will cover both her existing debts and the new machine. Evaluate whether Farida should take the bank loan (Option 1) or obtain £40,000 from a venture capitalist (Option 2). (Past paper, January 2024)
MARK SCHEME:
Possible figures:
- Existing debt: £15,000 + 12% (£1,800) = £16,800, or £1,400 a month.
- New loan: £25,000 × 15% = £3,750 interest in year 1 (£312.50 a month) + about £416.67 a month capital = about £729 a month for 5 years.
- Year 1 total: £1,400 + £729 = £2,129 a month, about £25,548 a year, when she made a £26,310 loss.

For the bank loan: she keeps control and all her profit; after year 1 only about £729 a month (£8,748 a year), which may cost less than giving away 50% of profit.

Against the loan: another monthly cost when she's making losses; she must find about £25,548 in 12 months; risk of insolvency or losing the business if she can't keep up.

For venture capital: no monthly repayments for either debt; gets the machine straight away; advice and help running the business; VCs are more willing to take risks than banks.

Against venture capital: gives away 50% of profit for 10 years; may lose the independence of being her own boss; possible conflict.

A strong answer uses the figures and reaches a supported judgement.

Levels: 10 to 12 thorough, balanced evaluation with a supported conclusion; 7 to 9 balanced with some support; 4 to 6 some application and judgement; 1 to 3 basic description.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic D1.2): Tom sells milkshakes from a mobile van and plans to sell them from vending machines at 10 locations in nearby towns. He can either buy second-hand machines for £1,200 each or lease brand new models from a national supplier for £40 per machine per week, for a minimum of three years. The £40 includes use of the location, electricity and security. If Tom bought his own machines, the location, electricity and security would cost £20 per week per machine. Option 1: a bank loan for all 10 machines, with interest for the four years charged at 25% of the total borrowed. Option 2: leasing the machines from the national supplier. Evaluate whether Tom should take the bank loan (Option 1) or lease the vending machines (Option 2). (Past paper, January 2025)
MARK SCHEME:
Option 1 figures: loan £12,000 + 25% interest (£3,000) = £15,000 ÷ 48 = £312.50 a month. Location etc: £20 × 52 × 10 = £10,400 a year (£866.67 a month). Total about £1,179.17 a month (£14,150 a year).

Option 2 figures: £40 × 52 × 10 = £20,800 a year = £1,733.33 a month.

For Option 1: owns the machines, which can be sold later; about £554 a month (£6,650 a year) cheaper; after 4 years only location costs remain.

Against Option 1: second-hand machines may break down, causing lost income and repairs; 4-year commitment; time to get the loan; machines may be worth little after 4 years.

For Option 2: only a 3-year commitment; new machines are less likely to break, so cash flow is more predictable; can hand them back if the venture fails; easier budgeting; no capital tied up; no depreciation; quicker to arrange.

Against Option 2: never owns the machines, so pays indefinitely; no asset to sell; pays for damage; expensive early termination charges.

A strong answer uses the figures and reaches a supported judgement.

Levels: 10 to 12 thorough, balanced evaluation with a supported conclusion; 7 to 9 balanced with some support; 4 to 6 some application and judgement; 1 to 3 basic description.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic D1.2): Jisoo runs The Circle, which sells redesigned and recycled retro clothing online and through a small shop. In 2022 her profit before tax was £7,215.50. She wants to expand the online side by moving production to a £150,000 unit in a Local Enterprise Partnership area supported by the UK Government. Grants of between £10,000 and £500,000 are available, with larger grants for businesses that take on workers who have been unemployed for more than six months. Option 1: apply for a grant from the Local Enterprise Partnership. Option 2: take out a £150,000 mortgage at a variable interest rate of 6.9% a year, repaid over 25 years. Evaluate whether Jisoo should apply for a grant (Option 1) or take out a mortgage (Option 2) to pay for the unit. (Past paper, January 2023)
MARK SCHEME:
Figures: interest on £150,000 at 6.9% is about £10,350 in year 1. A repayment mortgage would cost about £1,050 a month (around £12,600 a year), more than her 2022 profit of £7,215.50.

For the grant:
- Doesn't need repaying and no interest, so no extra outflows.
- Could cover the whole £150,000 if she qualifies for a large grant.
- Taking on long-term unemployed workers could help her expand production and supports the local community.

Against the grant:
- Not guaranteed; competitive and slow to apply for.
- May only get £10,000, leaving a big gap.
- Conditions: hiring long-term unemployed workers may need training and supervision, and adds wage costs.
- Must stay in the area or meet targets, or may have to repay.

For the mortgage:
- Raises the full £150,000 and she owns the unit, an asset that may rise in value.
- Spread over 25 years.
- Her decision alone, no conditions on who she hires.

Against the mortgage:
- Repayments of about £12,600 a year are more than her current profit.
- Variable rate: repayments rise if interest rates rise.
- Secured on the unit, which can be repossessed.
- Total repaid over 25 years is about £315,000.

A strong answer uses the figures and reaches a supported judgement, e.g. apply for the grant first and use a smaller mortgage for any gap.

Levels: 10 to 12 thorough, balanced evaluation using the figures with a supported conclusion; 7 to 9 balanced with some support; 4 to 6 some application and judgement; 1 to 3 basic description.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic F3): Cody wants to diversify Cody's Chauffeurs and improve its financial performance. He is considering buying either Option 1, a florist business, or Option 2, a wedding cake business. Evaluate whether Cody's Chauffeurs should buy the florist business or the wedding cake business to improve its financial performance. (Past paper, June 2025)

Year ending 31 Dec 2025 | Florist | Wedding cake
Sales revenue | £160,000 | £67,500
Profit margin | 25% | 40%
Profit for the year | £40,000 | £27,000
Trade receivable days | 30 | 60
Trade payable days | 0 | 30
Current ratio | 0.6 | 2
Business purchase price | £39,000 | £25,000
MARK SCHEME:
Florist:
- Higher revenue (£160,000) and profit (£40,000); pays back its £39,000 price in about a year (103% return).

Wedding cake:

A strong answer reaches a justified conclusion, e.g. the wedding cake business for its better margin, liquidity and lower price, or the florist for higher absolute profit if Cody can fix its liquidity.

QUESTION (12 marks, topic F5): Candy has decided to move into a new studio. Rent will be 40% higher than her existing premises, but she will have more space to attract bigger contracts. Candy feels that offering 60 days' credit has been important in increasing sales. Many competitors offer 30 days' credit and she is wondering if she should do the same, as the business goes overdrawn frequently. She has an agreed £10,000 overdraft at 17% APR with no other fees. Evaluate whether Candy should reduce her customers' credit period from 60 days to 30 days or continue to use an overdraft in order to manage her cash flow. (Past paper, June 2024)

Candy's Creative, 2023 | £
Current assets | 12,000
Current liabilities | 10,000
Total revenue | 175,000
Cost of sales | 63,000
Expenses | 45,000
Average selling price | 5,000
Trade receivables | 19,200
Credit sales | 32,000
