=== KEY POINTS: E1 Cash flow forecasts (Section B: Business finance) ===
A cash flow forecast predicts the money coming into and going out of a business over the coming months.

Inflows (receipts): cash sales, credit sales (when the customer actually pays), loans, capital introduced, sale of assets, bank interest received.

Outflows (payments): cash purchases, credit purchases (when the supplier is paid), rent, rates, salaries, wages, utilities, purchase of assets, VAT, bank interest paid.

Layout and formulas (not given in the exam, so learn them):
- Total inflows − total outflows = net cash flow
- Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow
- The closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next month.
- Negative figures are shown in brackets, e.g. (2,480). A negative closing balance means the business is overdrawn.

Always say whether a figure is positive or negative. In the exam a missing bracket loses the mark.

Revising a forecast: if a figure changes (e.g. wages fall by 25%), recalculate that figure, then total outflows, net cash flow, and every closing balance after it.

Uses: planning (see when money will be short and arrange an overdraft in advance), monitoring (compare actual with forecast), control (act on differences), target setting (set targets for sales or spending), securing finance (banks want to see one in a business plan).

Benefits: shows all inflows and outflows; good for planning and budgeting; shows when the business may go overdrawn so action can be taken; helps secure finance.
Limitations: it's a prediction so may be inaccurate; external changes (economy, competitors, costs) can't be predicted; only as good as the person who makes it; decisions may be based on wrong figures; takes time to prepare.

Improving cash flow:
- Increase inflows: raise prices (customers may buy less), promotions (cost money now), reduce the credit period given to customers (they may go elsewhere), sell unused assets, encourage cash sales.
- Reduce or delay outflows: delay buying assets, or use leasing or HP to spread the cost; negotiate longer credit from suppliers; find cheaper suppliers or premises; cut costs.
- Arrange an overdraft to cover short gaps.

Cash is not the same as profit: a profitable business can run out of cash if customers pay late or it buys expensive assets.

=== GLOSSARY: E1 Cash flow forecasts ===
- Net cash flow: Total inflows − total outflows.
- Closing balance: Opening balance + net cash flow.
- Opening balance: Last month's closing balance.
- Brackets in a forecast: Show a negative figure, e.g. (500) means −£500.
- Inflows: Cash sales, credit sales, loans, capital introduced, sale of assets, interest received.
- Outflows: Purchases, rent, rates, wages, salaries, utilities, assets, VAT, interest paid.
- Uses of a cash flow forecast: Planning, monitoring, control, target setting, securing finance.
- Limitation of a forecast: It's a prediction, so it may be inaccurate.

MARK SCHEME (Pearson mark scheme (condensed)):
1 mark for the impact and 1 for development, e.g.:
- Outflows increase (1), leading to lower net cash flow or closing balance (1).

- Higher inflows (VAT collected on sales) (1), leading to a higher net cash flow or closing balance (1).

QUESTION (2 marks): State one benefit and one limitation of using a cash flow forecast. (Past paper, January 2024)
MARK SCHEME (Pearson mark scheme (condensed)):
1 mark for a benefit and 1 for a limitation.

Benefits: aware of all inflows and outflows; good for planning and budgeting; shows if the business will go overdrawn; can take action to avoid deficits; can be used to get finance.

Limitations: it's a prediction so not always accurate; can't predict inflows and outflows exactly; owners may lack experience to interpret it; decisions may be based on inaccurate information.

'Time could be spent doing other things' gets no mark.

QUESTION (6 marks): Discuss actions that Arjun and Sai could take to improve the closing balances in the cash flow forecast in the first three months of 2024. (Past paper, January 2024)
DATA:
Home Office Creations | Jan £ | Feb £ | Mar £
Credit sales | 1,890 | 953 | 509
Cash sales | 20,068 | (ii) | 13,276
Bank interest |  |  | 98
Total inflows | 21,958 | 17,831 | 13,785
Purchase of assets |  | 15,500 | 
Rent | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500
Wages | 6,890 | 6,200 | 8,578
Utilities | 470 | 510 | (v)
Other outflows | 2,349 | 2,489 | 4,495
Total outflows | 11,209 | 26,199 | 14,953
Net cash flow | 10,749 | (iii) | (1,168)
Opening balance | (i) | (iv) | (5,008)
Closing balance | 3,360 | (5,008) | (vi)
MARK SCHEME (Pearson mark scheme (condensed)):
Increase inflows:
- Raise the selling price: only increases inflows if customers keep buying; they may not buy at a higher price.
- Advertising or promotion: may worsen cash flow in the short term, but increases the closing balance long term if it brings in more revenue than it costs.
- Reduce the credit period for customers: brings payments forward, but credit may be a sales tool, so customers may not buy at all.
- Other ways, e.g. a wider product range, with advantages and disadvantages.

Decrease outflows:
- Don't buy, or delay buying, the wood cutting machine: raises the closing balance by £15,500 in the short term, but no wage savings in the long term.
- Rent cheaper premises: relocating could be costly.
- Reduce other outflows.
- Buy the machine using HP, leasing or other credit to spread the cost.

Levels: 5 to 6 well-developed, balanced discussion in context; 3 to 4 partially developed, imbalanced; 1 to 2 basic description.

QUESTION (6 marks): Ruby has an overdraft facility charging 30% a year on outstanding balances. Benefits:
- Shows that buying the machine in September would leave her overdrawn by £1,117, so she can plan ahead.
- She can see the cost of using a 30% overdraft and look for cheaper options, e.g. buying the machine in a month with a bigger balance, or leasing.
- Helps monitor advertising, which rises sharply in November and December.
- Can be shown to the bank to support finance.
- Highlights months with low balances (e.g. November, £57).

- Based on estimates; pet treat sales may differ, e.g. seasonal demand.
- Assumes customers pay on time within 30 days.
- Doesn't show the efficiency savings the new machine may bring.
- External changes (ingredient costs, competitors) aren't predicted.
- Only as good as the person preparing it.
