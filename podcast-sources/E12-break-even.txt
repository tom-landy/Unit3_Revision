=== KEY POINTS: E2 Break-even analysis (Section B: Business finance) ===
Costs
- Fixed costs: don't change with output in the short term, e.g. rent, insurance, salaries, loan interest.
- Variable costs: change directly with output, e.g. raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages.
- Semi-variable costs: a fixed part plus a variable part, e.g. telephone, electricity and other utilities, vehicle costs, internet, wages with overtime.
- Total costs = fixed costs + total variable costs. Total variable costs = variable cost per unit × units.

Sales
- Total revenue (total sales) = selling price per unit × units sold.
- Sales can be measured in units or in value (£).

Formulas (not given in the exam)
- Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit
- Total contribution = contribution per unit × units sold (or total revenue − total variable costs)
- Break-even point (units) = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
- Break-even point (sales value) = break-even units × selling price
- Margin of safety (units) = actual or forecast sales − break-even point
- Margin of safety (value) = margin of safety in units × selling price
- Profit = total contribution − fixed costs (or total revenue − total costs)
- Units needed for a target profit = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit

Always round a break-even point UP to the next whole unit: 1,666.7 units means 1,667, because selling 1,666 still makes a loss.

In the exam, write the formula in words first, then the numbers, then the answer. Each can earn marks even if the final answer is wrong.

Break-even chart
- x-axis: output or sales (units). y-axis: costs and revenue (£).
- Fixed costs: a horizontal line.
- Total costs: starts where fixed costs meet the y-axis and rises.
- Total revenue: starts at zero and rises more steeply.
- Break-even point: where total revenue crosses total costs.
- Area of loss: left of the break-even point, between the lines. Area of profit: to the right.
- Margin of safety: the distance between actual sales and the break-even point.

What changes the break-even point?
- Higher selling price → higher contribution → lower BEP (but customers may buy less).
- Lower variable costs → higher contribution → lower BEP (but quality may suffer).
- Lower fixed costs → lower BEP (e.g. cheaper premises, but fewer customers or less marketing).

Uses: planning (is the idea viable?), what-if modelling of price or cost changes, setting sales targets, monitoring and controlling costs, supporting a loan application.
Limitations: assumes everything made is sold; assumes selling price and variable costs stay the same (no bulk discounts or price cuts); only works for one product; based on estimates; ignores external factors such as competitors; fixed costs can change (e.g. a rent rise).

=== GLOSSARY: E2 Break-even analysis ===
- Fixed costs: Don't change with output, e.g. rent, insurance.
- Variable costs: Change directly with output, e.g. raw materials.
- Semi-variable costs: Fixed part plus variable part, e.g. telephone, utilities.
- Contribution per unit: Selling price − variable cost per unit.
- Break-even point (units): Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
- Break-even point (value): Break-even units × selling price.
- Margin of safety: Actual or forecast sales − break-even point.
- Profit using contribution: Total contribution − fixed costs.
- Target profit output: (Fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit.
- Rounding the BEP: Always round up to the next whole unit.

Using their estimates for 2027, calculate (i) total costs at 2,000 t-shirts and (ii) total revenue at 4,000 t-shirts. Then work out the selling price, variable cost per t-shirt and break-even point. (Past paper, June 2025)
DATA:
Quantity | 0 | 2,000 | 4,000 | 6,000
Fixed costs (£) | 30,000 | 30,000 | 30,000 | 30,000
Total variable costs (£) | 0 | 10,000 | 20,000 | 30,000
Total costs (£) | 30,000 | (i) | 50,000 | 60,000
Total revenue (£) | 0 | 30,000 | (ii) | 90,000
MARK SCHEME (Mark scheme written by the teacher (no Pearson mark scheme available)):
On the paper (i) and (ii) are worth 2 marks; the chart is worth 4 more.
(i) £40,000 (1)
(ii) £60,000 (1)

From the table: selling price = £30,000 ÷ 2,000 = £15; variable cost = £10,000 ÷ 2,000 = £5; contribution = £10.
Break-even point = £30,000 ÷ £10 = 3,000 t-shirts (where total revenue and total costs cross on the chart, at £45,000) (2).

QUESTION (6 marks): Nesdi and Ben are considering buying a new printer, which would increase their expenses by £150 per month. It would improve the quality of their t-shirts and reduce the cost of printing each t-shirt to £4.50 (currently £5). Discuss the impact on T-hee-hee's profit for the year of buying the new printer. (Past paper, June 2025)
MARK SCHEME (Mark scheme written by the teacher (no Pearson mark scheme available)):
Figures: extra fixed costs £150 × 12 = £1,800 a year. Saving 50p per t-shirt. They need to sell 3,600 t-shirts (£1,800 ÷ £0.50) for the saving to cover the extra cost. At the 4,500 estimate, saving £2,250 − £1,800 = £450 more profit. At 6,000, £1,200 more profit. Below 3,600, profit falls.

New break-even: £31,800 ÷ £10.50 = 3,029 t-shirts (up from 3,000).

Other points:
- Better quality may allow a higher price or attract more customers, increasing profit further.
- Fewer misprints or wasted shirts.
- Risk: if sales are lower than forecast, the higher fixed cost reduces profit.
- Estimates may be wrong; the printer may need maintenance.

Levels: 5 to 6 well-developed, balanced discussion in context; 3 to 4 partially developed, imbalanced; 1 to 2 basic description.

QUESTION (6 marks): To increase profit, Johann plans to introduce a premium range of carpets which he will sell at £5,000 per roll. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of break-even analysis for Quality Threads Ltd. (Past paper, January 2026)
DATA:
Quality Threads Ltd | January 2026
Variable cost per roll of carpet | £570
Average revenue per roll of carpet | £2,600
Estimated number of rolls sold | 350
Rent of factory | £3,000
Other expenses | £1,030
Utilities | £2,060
MARK SCHEME (Pearson mark scheme (condensed)):
Advantages:
- Shows fixed and variable costs, so the impact of the premium range and higher variable costs can be analysed.
- Allows 'what if' modelling, e.g. the £5,000 selling price.
- Useful for planning different prices and variable costs.
- Helps set targets and judge whether the premium range will improve profit.
- Can help secure finance.
- Helps monitor and control costs and revenue.

Disadvantages:
- Based on predictions; customers may not pay £5,000 per roll.
- Assumes all carpets are sold.
- Ignores future changes in the cost of high-quality raw materials.
- Ignores external factors, e.g. competitors launching their own premium range.
- Only as reliable as the person who creates it.
- Only applies to one product.

Levels: 5 to 6 well-developed, balanced discussion in context; 3 to 4 partially developed, imbalanced; 1 to 2 basic description.
